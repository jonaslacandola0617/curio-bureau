# The Visual Language of Vox-Inspired Editorial Animation: An Art Direction and Motion Design Manual

**An evidence-based visual study of five supplied films, followed by a practical creative training manual.**  
Prepared 12 October 2026. Scope: art direction, visual explanation, animation choreography, and editorial judgment. No software implementation guidance.

## How to use this manual

Read Layer 1 to understand the actual examples and their differences. Read Layer 2 to learn the transferable design grammar. Use the scene-direction framework before designing a sequence, the pattern library while developing alternatives, and the quality checklist before approving it. Reference codes link the training principles to the chronological analyses below.

The central finding is that these films repeatedly **preserve something recognizable while changing the viewer's understanding of it**. A golf ball becomes an explanation of materials; a state becomes a population, an allocation of votes, and a measure of influence; an archival map becomes a simplified political diagram. Their sophistication comes from choosing the right representation and controlling its evolution, not from applying a fixed collection of transitions.

## Evidence, scope, and limitations

The supplied MP4 files were examined directly through timestamped chronological frame samples, approximately six seconds apart across each complete file, with fourteen selected passages examined at approximately half-second intervals. This establishes broad sequence coverage and much closer evidence for the choreography described in the motion studies. It is not a frame-by-frame audit of every cut. Small graphics in overview frames are not treated as reliably legible evidence.

All five files contain audio tracks. **Audio was not audibly reviewed or transcribed in this environment.** Exact spoken wording, word-level synchronization, music, sound effects, audio transitions, and intentional silence remain unverified. Visible subtitles support some narrative interpretation, particularly in the wildlife film. Elsewhere, narrative purpose is inferred from visible labels, charts, documents, and image order. Each sequence's synchronization note identifies the conceptual relationship to test; it does not claim a verified spoken cue. This manual provides an audio review method, not an invented soundtrack analysis.

Broad sequence boundaries are approximate editorial groupings, usually within several seconds of a change. Half-second studies locate events more closely, but their timestamps are still approximate. No specific easing curve, exact acceleration, motion-blur setting, or subframe timing has been measured. “Camera” means the apparent change in viewpoint or framing, not a claim about production technique. Interpretations of designer intent are labeled as rationale, not attributed statements by the original creators.

**Evidence notation:**

- **O — observed:** a visible composition, annotation, or change between inspected frames.
- **M — motion study:** ordered, closely spaced frames support the stated choreography.
- **I — interpretation:** a plausible communicative purpose inferred from the visual evidence.
- **T — transfer:** a recommendation for original work, not a claim about what the source did.
- **U — unverified:** exact sound, speech timing, easing, or an event between sampled frames.

This is an aesthetic analysis of historical media, not a current factual briefing on their topics. Dates, boundaries, statistics, medical claims, and political classifications appearing in the videos are analyzed as editorial representations. Reusing those claims would require separate current research. In particular, the wildlife film's animal-to-human chain should not be reused as a verified account of the origin of COVID-19; this study only establishes what the film depicts. “Today” in an older reference belongs to that film's publication context.

# LAYER 2 — SYNTHESIZED CREATIVE TRAINING

## 6. Aesthetic philosophy: what these films have in common

### 6.1 The image performs a reasoning operation

The strongest graphic sequences do not merely put a noun on screen. They compare, isolate, accumulate, convert, connect, locate, or reveal a consequence.

In E04, fixed state columns survive changes in the representation of political units. In G08, opposing positions make a trade-off visible, and convergence proposes a combination. In W10, an expanding group becomes a supplier within a larger system. In F04, fixed physical endpoints receive different numbers. Each sequence contains an operation that a static stock image could not communicate as clearly.

**Design test:** complete the sentence, “After this change, the viewer can now see ______.” If the answer is only “the scene looks more energetic,” the movement is not doing explanatory work. Decorative movement may still be appropriate for a title or brand ending, but it should not be mistaken for the core grammar.

### 6.2 Richness comes from evidence and relationships

These references are not uniformly sparse. The archive in I is dense. Television clips contain many competing marks. Newspaper pages are typographically complex. Animal groups in W become crowded. The shared discipline is selective attention: a frame, highlight, crop, label, or change of scale tells the viewer what matters now.

Minimalism therefore means **economy of the current reading task**, not an empty frame or primitive asset quality. A photograph may contain thousands of details while the task is simply to notice one highlighted sign. Conversely, six identical animated cards can feel overloaded if each asks for a different interpretation.

### 6.3 The representation changes when the question changes

A map is not the default answer to every historical question. E moves from maps to unit grids because geography cannot show allocation adequately. G leaves sports footage for a cross-section because footage cannot expose construction. W uses interview testimony when an explanation needs attribution, then diagrams when relationships need organization.

A future agent should name the question before selecting the format. “What exists where?” suggests a map. “How much?” suggests a quantity encoding. “How does this lead to that?” suggests a sequence or system diagram. “What did this person or document claim?” suggests attributed source material.

### 6.4 Continuity can be conceptual, spatial, or material

The references use several different kinds of continuity:

| Continuity type | Reference evidence | What remains stable | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Object continuity | G07–G11 | Circular ball identity | Exterior, material, performance, interior |
| Comparison continuity | E04–E05 | Left/right state identities | Units, totals, denominator, apparent weight |
| System continuity | W07–W14 | Government, animals, market roles | Scale, flows, restrictions, branches |
| Spatial continuity | I01, I06, I10 | A larger archival workspace or coastline | Selected source, framing, date, territory |
| Measurement continuity | F04, F07 | Physical endpoints or ordered axis | Labels and selected calibration point |
| Editorial continuity | W05, G04, E15 | The current question | Footage, testimony, diagram, document |

A transition feels natural when the preserved element helps the viewer understand why the next image belongs. The whole frame does not need to morph. A direct cut can preserve an argument more clearly than a spectacular transformation.

### 6.5 Visual pleasure comes from controlled discovery

The viewer repeatedly receives just enough structure to predict what comes next, followed by a meaningful revision. Equal conversion bars prepare the contrast with irregular bars. A familiar ball prepares the surprise of its interior. A state comparison becomes a different comparison when its denominator changes. Good staging makes discovery feel intuitive without making the explanation trivial.

## 7. Visual design system

### 7.1 Composition: design relationships before filling space

**Paired comparisons.** E04–E05 use two stable columns and generous separation. Names remain near their entities; captions sit beneath. The empty center accommodates annotations without disturbing the pair. For original work, align the baseline or center appropriate to the quantity, reserve space for the conclusion, and avoid letting a larger label imply a larger value.

**Central specimen.** G10–G11 use a ball against a dark technical field. Peripheral lines, arcs, and labels refer inward. This works when the viewer needs to inspect one object through successive states. Give the object enough room to enlarge before positioning callouts; otherwise every reveal forces a distracting layout reset.

**Directional system.** W10 organizes authority vertically and supply horizontally. Direction has meaning: above authorizes below; left supplies right. Choose orientations that remain stable for the entire explanation. If the layout changes, visibly reframe it before changing the relationships.

**Source workspace.** I treats sources as objects in a larger environment. The current source becomes dominant through framing, not by forcing every item into identical cards. Use the environment only when source relationships or historical accumulation matter. A desk surface is not a substitute for research or composition.

**Graphic over footage.** G02 and F01 place a small number of large, high-contrast marks over real images. The overlay should have its own clear plane. It need not pretend to sit physically in the scene. Place it away from faces, action paths, and essential evidence.

### 7.2 Static and animated composition are different problems

A good final frame does not guarantee a readable sequence. A designer must also account for the space needed during entry, comparison, and departure.

W10 makes room for a market box by reducing and shifting the established animal group. F03 moves the first chart upward before introducing the second. In both, reframing is a preparatory action. It tells the eye where the next information will fit.

For each scene, draw at least three compositions: the first readable state, the most informative state, and the resolved state. Check all three independently. Then ask whether the journey between them preserves object identity. Do not build an attractive final collage and improvise entrances afterward.

### 7.3 Foreground, background, and depth

There are at least three distinct depth systems in the references:

1. **Flat analytical depth:** marks sit on a stable page or grid. E is the clearest example. Hierarchy comes from size, contrast, grouping, and annotation.
2. **Object depth:** the ball in G has surface shading and dimensional cues while labels remain flat. Depth helps recognition and material understanding.
3. **Archival depth:** I uses borders, overlap, apparent perspective, and a shared surface to make sources feel arranged in space.

Do not apply all three indiscriminately. A chart does not need a heavy drop shadow on every bar. An archival photo can retain a border without becoming a floating card with exaggerated depth. Depth should clarify what is an object, what is an annotation, and what is a shared reference surface.

### 7.4 Negative space is capacity for explanation

Open space in E accommodates comparison and annotation. The pale sea area in I holds legends and supporting text. The dark field around G's ball makes tiny airflow strokes readable. W's cream diagrams leave space for the next system component.

This suggests a practical rule: reserve space for the next explanatory action, not only for a pleasing screenshot. Empty space can be temporarily unoccupied because the viewer is about to use it to understand a connection.

### 7.5 Color: separate identity, quantity, and attention

| Color role | Observed examples | Transfer rule |
|---|---|---|
| Persistent entity/category | Red/blue election graphics in E; colored regions in I | Keep meaning stable within the sequence and provide a legend when needed |
| Material or property | Blue/pink performance language and colored ball layers in G | Tie the color to a named property; do not reuse it casually for unrelated emphasis |
| Selection | Yellow document highlighting in W, E, G, F; orange rings in W | Use briefly and selectively so it points to the current reading task |
| Historical state or comparison | Changing map overlays in W and F | Display date, category, and scope with the change |
| Environment | Cream grids, charcoal fields, warm wood | Keep contrast sufficient; background identity must not obscure evidence |

A frequent mistake is to make the brightest color mean both “this category” and “look here.” If the blue series is the subject of the sentence, keep it blue and use a different selection device, such as an outline or isolation of other series. Do not recolor it into a new semantic category just to attract attention.

The films do not share one universal palette. An original project should choose its own constrained system. The transferable principle is stable assignment of roles, not copying a recognizable publisher's yellow or exact red/blue pair.

### 7.6 Typography: four jobs, not one animated headline style

**Identity text** names people, places, objects, or categories. It should remain attached perceptually to its referent. E's state names and G's object labels do this.

**Explanatory text** supplies what the image cannot state alone: a denominator, date, policy name, or comparison criterion. Its precision matters more than its theatrical entrance.

**Emphasis text** briefly identifies the important word or value. Large temperatures in F and highlighted phrases in E are examples. It should not force the viewer to read a full sentence in enormous type while also decoding a chart.

**Source text** belongs to a document, headline, or quotation. Preserve its attribution and enough surrounding context. A highlight should reveal the argument within the source, not convert the source into an unattributed slogan.

Visible typographic differences matter. E often uses serif headings and labels that suggest a printed analytical page. W uses heavier, more condensed uppercase diagram labels. F and G use bold sans-serif headlines and numerical emphasis alongside source typography. I mixes source-native type, uppercase map labels, and historical/date treatments. No single headline family can stand in for this whole corpus.

### 7.7 Typography in motion

The inspected sequences support several behaviors: labels appear after a visual structure is established; a large value follows its descriptive label; a highlight arrives after readable text; old labels clear when their referents change. They do not support a universal claim that all text bounces, types on, or slides from a particular direction.

A label may arrive quickly, but it must become stationary enough to read. Moving every word independently converts typography into competing objects. Animate the hierarchy: heading, entity, relation, emphasis. Let most letterforms remain intact.

When labels change meaning, make the replacement explicit. E05 needs a new explanatory caption because square size no longer stands for the previous quantity. Typography is part of the data encoding, not a finishing layer.

### 7.8 Imagery: unify by role rather than forcing one treatment

W's animal illustrations are more descriptive than generic dots because species differences matter. E's black squares work because interchangeable countable units matter. G's realistic ball surface matters when discussing dimples; a simple concentric diagram matters when discussing construction. I's source borders matter because historical documents are being examined as sources.

Choose detail according to explanatory need. An overly generic animal symbol erases distinctions. An overly detailed electoral token makes a count harder to scan. A detailed building or vehicle is useful only when its recognizable structure serves the story; decorative detail should not consume attention required by a relation or quantity.

Unify mixed imagery through consistent scale logic, framing, label placement, contrast, and recurring environments. Do not recolor every photograph simply because the graphic palette is limited. Documentary evidence often benefits from retaining its own visual character.

### 7.9 Texture and the illusion of material

Graph paper in F and G suggests measurement and inspection. Pale page texture in E softens the analytical surface without dominating it. Wood, paper, clips, and borders in I establish the archive. W uses relatively clean cream surfaces for systems and darker fields for reportage and maps.

Textures support a mode of thought. They are not proof of sophistication by themselves. If a texture interferes with fine lines, small labels, or tonal distinctions in source imagery, reduce it. A background should not be the most active thing during an explanation.

## 8. Motion design grammar

### 8.1 A useful sentence structure for motion

Describe a motion beat as:

**Known state → meaningful operation → newly readable state → hold or next question.**

Examples from the references:

- E04: two state allocations → symbol conversion and added units → a new allocation comparison → hold.
- W10: small animal group → accumulation and transfer → a supply system → hold.
- G08: separated performance options → convergence and enlargement → a combined cross-section → selective emphasis.
- F04: fixed physical endpoints → additional numeric labels → two scales for the same interval → hold.

This is more useful than “slide in, zoom, fade out.” The operation determines what must remain fixed, what can move, and when the viewer is ready for the next change.

### 8.2 Choreography is a hierarchy of dependencies

In a readable sequence, some events depend on earlier ones being understood. A label identifying a layer is premature before the layer is visible. A restriction mark is meaningless before the system being restricted has been established. A comparison needs both cases before its difference can be emphasized.

W10 visibly uses these dependencies: existing group, additional animals, reframing, market destination, transfer, connector. G11 establishes the cross-section before adding leader labels. F07 arrives at each scale location before finalizing the large number. These are not arbitrary staggers; each delay enables comprehension.

For original work, distinguish three kinds of overlap:

- **Helpful overlap:** a departing background recedes while the new main object begins to establish itself.
- **Dependent overlap:** a connector grows toward a destination that is arriving, but the viewer can still follow the relationship.
- **Competing overlap:** a camera move, a complex graph change, and a paragraph reveal all demand attention at once.

Use the first two deliberately. Redesign the third unless the simultaneous relationship is itself the subject.

### 8.3 Direction should mean something

Rightward transfer in W10 corresponds to supply moving toward a market. Upward travel in F07 selects a higher point on a temperature scale. Lateral travel in I10 connects adjacent evidence and consequences. Convergence in G08 combines alternatives.

A future agent should be able to explain the direction in a sentence. If any direction would communicate equally well, choose the one that best preserves layout and reading order, and keep its visual intensity modest.

Do not make a number move upward merely because the narration sounds exciting. If upward motion can be read as growth, it carries a quantitative implication whether intended or not.

### 8.4 Motion personality and perceived weight

The close studies show different personalities:

| Personality | Evidence | What creates it | Suitable transfer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Analytical | E04–E07 | Fixed columns, progressive unit changes, long resolved holds | Allocation, comparison, changing categories |
| Procedural | W04, W10 | Staged nodes, directed connectors, accumulation, transfer | Systems, supply chains, sequences |
| Inspectable object | G08, G11 | Stable circular identity, enlargement, selective layer reveal | Materials, anatomy, product mechanisms |
| Guided navigation | I01, I06, I10 | Viewpoint travel across related sources, pauses on evidence | Historical archives, document-led explanations |
| Demonstrative | F03, F04, F07 | Simple measurement objects, ordered stops, clear labels | Standards, measurement, comparison |

“Weight” here is perceptual. A source page feels placed because it has a border, overlap, and a stable surface. A ball feels like an object because its shading and surface remain coherent. A data square feels symbolic because it changes without pretending to obey physical forces.

Do not give every category of element the same elastic entrance. In these studies, broad recurring spring-bounce behavior is not established. Overshoot, anticipation, and blur must be observed or justified for the new subject; they should not be added merely because an animation feels too plain.

### 8.5 Timing: action time and understanding time

The inspected motion often resolves well before the sequence ends. W04's chain is substantially complete around 01:57 and remains readable into the following seconds. E05's resized state pair holds for several seconds before the oval annotation develops. G08 pauses with two alternatives separated before they converge. These holds are part of the animation design.

A useful planning distinction is:

- **Action time:** the time needed to perceive a change.
- **Recognition time:** the time needed to identify the new state.
- **Inference time:** the time needed to understand what the change means.
- **Reading time:** any additional time required by labels, values, or source text.

They may overlap, but none disappears simply because the movement is fast. A scene can be visually active while cognitively stalled, or almost still while delivering the main insight.

| Passage | Approximate visible order | Transferable lesson |
|---|---|---|
| E 01:41–01:43 | Circle-to-square conversion progresses through the left grid | A wave of change can preserve individual positions while updating a category |
| E 01:57–01:59, then 02:04–02:05 | State tiles resize; a later oval emphasizes a caption | Separate structural change from conclusion emphasis |

These are observation windows, not universal duration presets. Half-second sampling supports sequence order and rough duration, not exact ease curves. A new scene should be timed to its own reading burden and narration.

### 8.7 Camera movement versus object movement

Ask which coordinate system should remain trustworthy.

If the viewer is comparing quantities, keep the comparison frame stable and change the marks inside it. E does this effectively in the two-state study. If the viewer is exploring a larger source arrangement, move the viewpoint while the arrangement stays coherent, as in I10. If the viewer is inspecting a single object, maintain its identity and use selective enlargement or layer changes, as in G11.

Moving both the camera and the relevant objects can make change ambiguous. Did a bar grow or did the view zoom? Did a region expand or did the map reframe? A new design should separate these operations or include a stable visual reference that disambiguates them.

### 8.8 Exits are part of explanation

An exit can mean “no longer relevant,” “absorbed into a larger category,” “moved to a new role,” or simply “the scene is over.” These meanings are different.

W04 removes earlier pathogen examples as the final chain becomes the focal model. F03 retains the first chart by moving it upward because comparison still needs it. G11 removes a material layer while keeping the core because the remaining structure is now the subject. E06 compresses the categorical state field into context for the next explanation.

Before directing an exit, state whether the information should remain available to memory, remain physically visible, or be intentionally excluded. Do not fade away a baseline that the next comparison depends on.

### 8.9 Decoration versus communication

Consider two possible treatments of a supply relationship:

- All icons gently float while arrows pulse indefinitely. This creates activity but does not identify what changes or why.
- Animals first form a group, then move into a destination box, after which a stable arrow records the relationship. This gives movement a semantic role, as observed in W10.

Decoration can establish mood or signal branding. It becomes harmful when it obscures the causal event, creates false quantitative implications, or prevents the resolved graphic from becoming readable.

## 9. Editorial language: selecting and connecting visual modes

### 9.1 Match the evidence form to the claim

| If the viewer needs to understand… | Prefer… | Reference example | Avoid… |
|---|---|---|---|
| What a real action looks like | Footage, with selective annotation | G02 | Replacing useful evidence with a generic icon |
| Where an event occurs | A map with relevant landmarks | W02, I09 | A map used only as atmospheric wallpaper |
| How quantities differ | A clearly labeled comparative graphic | E05, F03 | Numbers floating without a shared scale or denominator |
| How a process connects entities | A staged system diagram | W10 | Presenting the final network before its parts have meaning |
| What an object is made of | A specimen and cross-section | G07, G11 | Revealing layers before the exterior is recognized |
| What a document says | Whole source, readable detail, selective highlight | W09, F10 | An illegible page used as proof-like texture |
| How conditions overlap in time | A timeline with duration bands | E12 | A row of dates that hides duration |
| Who is making an interpretation | Attributed testimony | W05, G12 | Unattributed quotation masquerading as established fact |
| What changed historically | A stable base with dated states | I06–I09, F09 | A beautiful morph with no temporal or categorical labels |

### 9.2 Vary cognitive work, not just shot size

The references alternate different tasks: observe footage, read a short label, compare two shapes, inspect a source, follow a path, hear or read testimony. This variety is deeper than changing from wide to close shots.

A strong sequence plan marks the task required by each beat. If five consecutive scenes all require reading paragraphs, changing their backgrounds will not solve the fatigue. If every scene is a rapid montage, the viewer never gets to construct a model. Alternate discovery, explanation, evidence, and reflection according to the story.

### 9.3 Complexity determines pace

W05 and W13 allow sustained interview-led passages. I uses relatively extended archival navigation and event cards. E spends time with comparison layouts and timelines. G's opening sports passage differs from its slower material explanation. F can move briskly among familiar measurement objects, then stage a scale step by step.

Therefore, “change something every second” is not a reference-derived rule. Neither is “never hold a static frame.” The better rule is: introduce a new visual demand when the current demand has become understandable or when the narration explicitly opens a new question.

### 9.4 Elaborate transitions have a cost

A sophisticated transition may require the viewer to track identity, orientation, scale, and changing meaning at once. It is justified when that tracking is the explanation, as in G08's convergence or I06's source-map handoff.

Use a direct cut when the next image is a different kind of evidence, when time must clearly jump, or when a transition would imply a relationship stronger than the evidence supports. W's interview and footage alternation demonstrates that coherence does not require every scene to become the next scene physically.

### 9.5 Audio: what remains unverified and how to direct it responsibly

The supplied audio tracks were not reviewed. No recurring whoosh, click, paper sound, music genre, beat-synchronized cut, or silence pattern is asserted in this manual.

For a future original film, the following are **creative recommendations, not reference findings**:

1. Identify the noun, relationship, and conclusion in each spoken passage. Establish the noun visually before asking the viewer to interpret a change to it.
2. Let the decisive visual operation coincide with the relevant concept, not necessarily the first syllable of every word. A connector can begin while the relation is introduced and resolve as its consequence is stated.
3. Avoid making a new paragraph, unfamiliar chart, and important spoken claim arrive simultaneously. Let one establish context for the other.
4. Use sound to clarify a material or editorial event only where it helps. A layer opening may merit a subtle cue; a growing statistical quantity need not acquire an invented physical impact.
5. Do not make the volume or force of a sound imply a larger quantitative difference than the graphic shows.
6. Allow a comparison or important quotation to remain readable without continuous sonic punctuation.
7. Review the film once with narration alone, once without sound, and once with the complete mix. The three passes expose different failures.

To complete a source-specific audio audit, review the fourteen close-study windows and the major scene boundaries while listening. Record whether speech leads, coincides with, or follows each visual event; whether sound is diegetic, editorial, or musical; and whether a cut is motivated by audio or by visual logic. Until that pass exists, all exact synchronization claims remain U.

## 11. Reusable motion and visual pattern library

All patterns below have visible reference evidence. The proposed variations are original applications, not additional source observations. Exact easing and sound are unspecified unless directly established. Choose a pattern because it fits the reasoning operation, not because a scene needs an effect.

### P01 — The delayed highlight

**Category:** typography and evidence reading.  
**Behavior/construction:** readable text occupies a stable source or quotation field; one phrase receives a colored highlight, oval, or underline.  
**Personality/choreography:** establish context first, then add a short, directed act of selection; preserve the surrounding text long enough to understand the selected phrase.  
**Use:** identify the clause or value that changes the argument.  
**Evidence:** W09 around 04:24–04:36; E05 around 02:04–02:05; F10 around 02:45–02:47. E05 and F10 have closer timing evidence.  
**Variation:** circle a denominator in a report, underline a date that resolves a contradiction, or emphasize a limiting word in a quotation.  
**Mistakes:** highlighting everything; beginning before the source is readable; removing attribution; making the highlight imply endorsement rather than selection.

### P02 — The value arrives after the condition

**Category:** typographic explanation.  
**Behavior/construction:** a physical or conceptual condition is pictured and labeled; a larger value then completes it.  
**Personality/choreography:** icon or event establishes meaning, descriptive label identifies it, number supplies precision, then the composition holds.  
**Use:** calibration, thresholds, definitions, scientific or historical reference points.  
**Evidence:** F07 around 01:56–02:07, especially freezing and body-temperature stops.  
**Variation:** establish an empty reservoir before showing its capacity, or show a boundary condition before a threshold value.  
**Mistakes:** an enormous unexplained number first; tiny units; a count-up that implies measurement over time when the number is simply a static fact.

### P03 — The source becomes a readable detail

**Category:** photographic/document treatment.  
**Behavior/construction:** a whole document establishes identity, followed by a close crop of a relevant passage.  
**Personality/choreography:** contextual view, deliberate selection, settled detail, then optional highlight.  
**Use:** evidentiary quotations, legislation, technical reports, archival headlines.  
**Evidence:** W09 04:23–04:36; F10 02:46–03:00. Exact zoom paths are not verified throughout these intervals.

### P04 — The evidence stack

**Category:** layered composition.  
**Behavior/construction:** differently proportioned sources overlap within a consistent environment; the current source is larger, clearer, or more central.  
**Personality/choreography:** accumulation with selective attention rather than equal prominence for every layer.  
**Use:** show a public conversation, a historical period, or multiple kinds of evidence.

### P05 — Cutout plus measurement

**Category:** photography and information graphics.  
**Behavior/construction:** a recognizable person or object overlaps a measurable graphic while labels and endpoints remain legible.

Persistent decorative background animation is not established as a shared pattern in this inspection. ## 12. Creative production workflow for a new script

### Step 1 — Identify the argument and the viewer's starting model

Write the film's main claim in one sentence. Then write the plausible but incomplete idea the audience may begin with. Identify where the story changes that idea.

For each paragraph, classify its job: premise, evidence, mechanism, comparison, qualification, consequence, or resolution. Mark facts needing precise labels and claims that must remain attributed. Note emotional changes separately from factual changes. A distressing event may call for quieter graphics, but emotional gravity does not determine a chart's scale.

**Output:** a list of narrative beats, each containing one question the viewer must be able to answer afterward.

### Step 2 — Establish a subject-appropriate visual direction

Choose a limited set of visual modes. For example: documentary photographs, a clean map, and a system diagram. State how they share typography, color roles, annotation style, and framing.

Choose a motion personality that fits the argument: analytical, procedural, exploratory, or object-focused. These can coexist, but each should belong to a reason. Decide what counts as a physical object, what counts as a symbolic mark, and what counts as an editorial annotation.

**Output:** a short art-direction sheet with roles, not just adjectives. “Dark technical field for internal mechanisms; cream evidence page for documents; orange only for selection” is actionable. “Premium, dynamic, cinematic” is not.

### Step 3 — Invent several visual concepts for each difficult idea

Generate at least two distinct representations for a major abstract claim. Compare them on clarity, factual fidelity, and continuity with adjacent beats.

A quantity comparison might use bars, units, or a distribution. A process might use a line of stages, a system diagram, or one object changing state. Ask what each representation reveals and what it hides. Prefer the concept that exposes the relevant relationship with the fewest unsupported assumptions.

**Output:** a chosen concept plus one sentence explaining why alternatives were rejected. Do not default to a metaphor if literal data or footage would be clearer.

### Step 4 — Plan successive readable states

Define the initial composition, the first new information, the operation, and the resolved composition. Name the invariant: object identity, location, baseline, scale, date, or category.

Specify what remains visible and what clears. If a graphic becomes denser, decide how space is made before the new information arrives. If the camera moves, state which orientation cues survive.

**Output:** a sequence of three to six key states described in visual terms. Each state should add information, not merely change layout.

### Step 5 — Choreograph attention and motion

Name the primary moving element and the supporting elements. Specify direction, order, overlap, relative speed, settling, and hold. State whether the movement is symbolic, physical, or a change of view.

Do not invent physical realism where none is needed. A chart mark should arrive at a trustworthy value. A source page should become readable. A ball's rotation should clarify surface or structure. These are different motion requirements.

**Output:** a beat-by-beat direction in which the viewer has one primary interpretive task at a time.

### Step 6 — Connect scenes by meaning

For every boundary, identify what the next image inherits: the object, a location, a line, a category, a question, or simply the narration's argument. Choose a continuous transition only if the inheritance benefits from being seen.

A direct cut is a valid design decision. Record why it is preferable when a change of evidence form, historical period, or emotional register should be clear.

**Output:** a transition sentence explaining the relationship between scenes, followed by its visual expression.

### Step 7 — Refine rhythm with actual reading demands

Review the sequence as a chain of cognitive tasks. Give unfamiliar diagrams more recognition time than familiar footage. Allow an important comparison to hold after motion resolves. Remove redundant labels that restate what the image already makes obvious.

When narration is available, check which visual event should precede, coincide with, or follow each key concept. Leave sound decisions open until the picture's purpose is clear.

**Output:** a timing plan based on comprehension, with explicit holds and a reason for each faster section.

### Step 8 — Review quality and revise the concept when needed

First ask whether the scene explains the intended idea. Only then inspect elegance, consistency, and polish. If the concept is wrong, better easing will not repair it.

Check the image without narration. Check narration without the image. Then check both together. If the visual contradicts a qualification in the words, revise the visual rather than expecting the viewer to overlook it.

**Output:** a short review listing the current takeaway, the strongest evidence, the main ambiguity, and the change required before approval.

## 13. Standardized scene-direction framework

Use this structure for every important original sequence. It is intentionally expressed in artistic and editorial terms.

1. **Narrative proposition:** the one thing the viewer should understand.
2. **Evidence status:** observed fact, attributed claim, hypothesis, illustrative example, or hypothetical scenario.
3. **Visual concept:** the representation and the reasoning operation it enables.
4. **Initial composition:** background, main subject, supporting evidence, text hierarchy, empty space, and viewpoint.
5. **Invariant:** what must remain recognizable or measurable throughout.
6. **Beat sequence:** for each beat, state what enters, moves, changes, remains, and clears.
7. **Motion character:** direction, relative pace, acceleration/settling intent, weight, overlap, and whether movement is symbolic or physical.
8. **Resolved state and hold:** the completed relationship and what the viewer needs time to read or infer.
9. **Narration relationship:** which concept each beat supports; mark exact word timing as pending until verified.
10. **Transition:** what connects the previous and next ideas, and why the chosen cut or transformation expresses that relationship.
11. **Art-direction rationale:** why the palette, image treatment, typography, and spatial organization fit the subject.
12. **Failure checks:** potential false implication, missing label, unreadable detail, competing movement, or unnecessary decoration.

### Example of insufficient direction

“Show a modern chart. Bars slide in smoothly. Zoom to a number. Add dynamic transitions.”

It does not specify a comparison, baseline, meaning of color, reason for motion, or what the audience learns.

### Example of useful direction

“Begin with two equally wide groups labeled by region. Show the same total for both and hold. Reveal the individual values within each group without moving the labels or the common scale. In the left group, three low values and one high value become visible; in the right, all values match. Add the word ‘average’ only after the shared value is established, then shift emphasis to the different distributions. Hold both groups together so the audience can see that an equal average does not imply an equal experience.”

This direction names the logic. Styling and motion can now support it instead of substituting for it.

## 14. Original application exercises

The following are original teaching designs, not descriptions of the supplied videos. Timings are proposed working durations, not measured reference timings. Narration is newly written. Quantities and the historical setting are explicitly synthetic so the exercises can demonstrate design reasoning without introducing unsupported factual claims.

### Exercise A — Abstract concept: hidden dependency

**Original narration:** “Three tasks look independent. But all three need the same approval. Finishing the work faster does not remove that bottleneck. The delay sits in the dependency, not in the tasks themselves.”

**Concept.** Use a small editorial workflow diagram whose shared dependency is initially hidden. This explains an abstract constraint through relationships, avoiding an elaborate machine metaphor that might imply physical throughput measurements.

**Initial composition.** Three vertically aligned paper-like task slips occupy the left half of a pale field. Each has a distinct short label and an empty completion box. The right half is open. Charcoal carries structure; muted blue identifies completed work; amber is reserved for the shared dependency. A modest sans-serif headline supplies context without repeating the narration.

| Proposed moment | Visual development and choreography | Narration relationship | Artistic rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–4 s | Establish the three slips, one after another; hold them in a common alignment | “Three tasks look independent” | Repetition suggests separate but comparable work; the hold lets the viewer form the initial model |
| 4–9 s | Extend a thin line from each slip toward the same amber approval node in the open right half | “all three need the same approval” | Convergence reveals the dependency; the node is introduced before the paths finish |
| 9–13 s | Add completion checks to all task slips; keep the approval node unresolved | “Finishing the work faster” | Completion is local to the tasks, so it does not incorrectly resolve the whole process |
| 13–18 s | Let the completed slips remain still; emphasize the single unresolved node with a brief outline | “does not remove that bottleneck” | Stillness lets the shared constraint become the reading task; no endless queue animation is needed |
| 18–23 s | Clear the slip details but retain three labeled inputs and the shared node; add a concise dependency label | Final sentence | Simplification turns the example into a reusable mental model |

**Motion personality.** Restrained, procedural, with clear connector direction. No bouncing papers or perpetual pulsing. The decisive event is the convergence of three paths into one dependency.

**Transition.** If the next scene discusses possible remedies, keep the node and change its arrangement explicitly. If it discusses a different case, use a direct cut after the resolved model holds.

**Transfer from references.** W10 supplies the principle of building a system in stages; E04 supplies stable comparison positions. This exercise uses different content, objects, and relationships.

**Critical review.** The graphic must not imply measured waiting times or actual processing rates. If the next claim is that adding another approver solves the problem, that claim needs evidence rather than an automatically multiplying node.

### Exercise B — Quantity comparison: the same average, different experiences

**Original narration:** “These two groups have the same average journey: twenty minutes. But in the first, three journeys take ten minutes and one takes fifty. In the second, every journey takes twenty. The average matches. The experience does not.”

**Synthetic data.** Group A: 10, 10, 10, 50 minutes. Group B: 20, 20, 20, 20 minutes. Each mean is 20 minutes. These are illustrative values, not a real survey.

**Concept.** Begin with equal summary values, then reveal the distributions that the summary hides. Use length on one common scale. Do not encode time with both icon size and bar length.

**Initial composition.** Two vertically stacked panels share the same left origin and minute scale. Each initially contains a single summary marker at 20. Group names sit at left; units sit above the shared scale. Ivory background, dark labels, slate bars, and one warm accent on the 50-minute journey keep the data hierarchy clear.

| Proposed moment | Visual development and choreography | Narration relationship | Artistic rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–4 s | Establish both summary markers at the same horizontal position; label 20 minutes and “average” | First sentence | Equality is visible before the complication |
| 4–10 s | Expand Group A vertically into four rows while preserving the scale and its summary marker; reveal three short bars, then the long bar | First group's values | The new information is distribution, not a new total; the long bar arrives after the repeated short pattern |
| 10–15 s | Expand Group B into four equal bars ending at 20 | Second group's values | Identical lengths provide the contrast without extra rhetoric |
| 15–19 s | Draw a subtle shared vertical reference at 20 across both panels | “The average matches” | Restores the original summary within the now-visible detail |
| 19–24 s | Add a small range annotation to each panel; hold the complete comparison | “The experience does not” | The conclusion is supported by visible spread, not merely an emphatic headline |

**Motion personality.** Exact, settled, and quiet. Bar endpoints must not overshoot values. The long bar's duration should not imply that its value is being measured live.

**Typography.** Values remain close to bar endpoints. The term “average” belongs to the summary reference; individual rows are not labeled as averages. The synthetic-data note remains small but readable.

**Transition.** Retain the 20-minute reference if the next scene discusses summaries. Retain the distributions if it discusses inequality or variability. Do not wipe away the comparison before the final sentence is understood.

**Transfer from references.** E05 demonstrates announcing a changed measure; E07 demonstrates retaining overview context while adding detail. This exercise introduces its own data and arrangement.

**Critical review.** Verify the arithmetic, common scale, equal group size, and label hierarchy. A dramatic color should not imply that the 50-minute observation is an error or morally “bad”; it is selected because it explains the spread.

### Exercise C — Historical/geographic cause and effect: a fictional bridge

**Original narration:** “In this imagined town, the two markets faced each other across a river. Traders still had to detour to the crossing upstream. When a bridge opened between the markets, the route shortened. The river stayed where it was. Access changed.”

**Status.** A fictional historical teaching example. No dates, distances, actual town, or empirical economic effects are asserted.

**Concept.** Keep the geography fixed while changing connectivity. Distinguish a shorter route from any unproven claim about trade growth or prosperity.

**Initial composition.** A simple overhead town plan fills the frame. A river runs vertically. One market lies on each bank near the center; the existing crossing lies near the top. Muted blue identifies water, charcoal marks streets, dark green marks the two markets, and amber identifies the selected travel route. A small “Illustrative town” label establishes status.

| Proposed moment | Visual development and choreography | Narration relationship | Artistic rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5 s | Establish river, banks, market markers, and the upstream crossing; keep the viewpoint fixed | “two markets faced each other” | The apparent closeness creates a question without hiding the barrier |
| 5–11 s | Trace a route from the left market upstream, across, and back down to the right market; retain the entire path | “had to detour” | The route reveals connectivity, not just distance between points |
| 11–15 s | Introduce a modest bridge shape between the markets; label it as the new crossing | “a bridge opened” | A single infrastructure change is isolated against a stable map |
| 15–20 s | Add the new short route; reduce the old route's contrast but leave it visible | “the route shortened” | Before and after coexist, avoiding reliance on memory |
| 20–25 s | Hold the river and both markets completely still; emphasize only the new connection | Final two sentences | The invariant geography makes changed access legible |

**Motion personality.** Deliberate route tracing with no camera move during comparison. The bridge should arrive as a symbolic addition rather than a spectacular construction sequence that distracts from connectivity.

**Typography and color.** Short labels identify old and new routes. Use differing line styles as well as contrast so the comparison remains understandable without color. Avoid a numerical distance claim unless a real scale is supplied.

**Transition.** A following scene about journey time could keep the two paths and introduce separately justified values. A scene about social effects should cut to appropriate evidence rather than automatically spawning shops or money symbols.

**Transfer from references.** G01 supplies place-to-route reasoning; I10 supplies event beside consequence; F04 supplies the discipline of keeping the reference constant while changing its interpretation.

**Critical review.** The map shows route shortening only. It does not prove economic growth, universal benefit, or the absence of displaced activity. Keep the inference as narrow as the evidence.

## 15. Creative direction rulebook

### A. Visual identity rules

1. Assign every major visual mode a job: evidence, explanation, comparison, or orientation.
2. Choose one coherent typographic hierarchy; allow source documents to retain their own typography within it.
3. Separate persistent category colors from temporary attention colors.
4. Use detail where recognition or evidence requires it. Use abstraction where count, relation, or category matters more.
5. Keep labels perceptually attached to their referents through reframing.
6. Give mixed imagery consistency through hierarchy and framing rather than a universal filter.
7. Use texture to establish a relevant material world; reduce it when it impairs reading.
8. Compose the beginning, middle, and resolution of a sequence, not only its final frame.

### B. Motion design rules

1. Name the reasoning operation before choosing the movement.
2. Identify the invariant and protect it.
3. Establish a structure before labeling its details.
4. Use stagger to reveal dependencies or categories, not as a default flourish.
5. Distinguish camera movement from change in the represented quantity.
6. Let numerical graphics settle on trustworthy values without expressive overshoot.
7. Keep physical behavior coherent when an object is presented as physical; do not impose physical behavior on every symbol.
8. Design the hold as carefully as the entrance.
9. Clear obsolete annotations before they attach to a changed meaning.
10. Let exits express relevance, transfer, or integration where appropriate.

### C. Visual storytelling rules

1. Ask what the audience must learn from the next image that it cannot learn from the current one.
2. Prefer the representation that exposes the relevant relationship with the fewest unsupported assumptions.
3. Show input, rule, and output when the mechanism matters.
4. Establish an exterior or familiar referent before opening an abstract interior.
5. Change legends and captions when the metric or category changes.
6. Mark hypotheses, proposals, historical states, and illustrative examples explicitly.
7. Preserve evidence attribution and meaningful qualifications.
8. Use metaphor to organize an argument; do not let it supply missing proof.
9. Revisit a familiar model when the conclusion depends on accumulated understanding.

### D. Editing and rhythm rules

1. Alternate cognitive tasks, not merely backgrounds or shot sizes.
2. Let complex comparisons remain visible after motion ends.
3. Use footage when observing the event is more informative than illustrating it.
4. Use direct cuts when continuity would imply an unsupported connection or obscure a time change.
5. Make elaborate transitions earn their attention cost.
6. Plan visual–narration alignment at the level of concepts, then verify actual wording and delivery.
7. Do not infer or prescribe reference-specific music and SFX without listening evidence.
8. Keep credits and promotion separate from the final explanatory beat.

### E. Artistic quality rules

1. The viewer should know what to inspect first without needing narration to rescue the hierarchy.
2. Every added layer should contribute evidence, relation, scale, context, or emotion appropriate to the argument.
3. Asset quality means appropriate specificity, not maximum detail everywhere.
4. A clean frame can still be intellectually weak; test the explanatory concept before polishing motion.
5. A dense frame can be clear if the current reading task is controlled.
6. Continuity should reduce the need to relearn the scene.
7. Data encodings must remain internally consistent through animation.
8. Any uncertainty that matters to the argument should be visible rather than hidden in a caveat outside the frame.

### F. Anti-patterns and corrections

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Every sentence becomes a fresh card | Destroys continuity and makes the film feel like narrated slides | Keep a model alive across several related claims |
| Constant zooming or drifting | Makes the reference frame unstable | Move only to select evidence, change scale, or connect related compositions |
| Generic icons for specific evidence | Removes the recognizable distinctions the story needs | Use a more precise illustration, photograph, or source image |
| High-detail assets without a visual argument | Adds production value without explanation | Name the relationship or change the detail supports |
| A final infographic revealed all at once | Forces the viewer to discover its structure unaided | Build entities and relations in a meaningful order |
| Same animation on every element | Erases hierarchy | Give the main change priority and keep support quieter |
| Unlabeled size transformation | Changes meaning without informing the viewer | Clear old labels, introduce the new metric, then resize |
| Color used inconsistently | Breaks learned categories | Maintain a semantic color ledger throughout the scene |
| Sources as decorative texture | Creates the appearance of evidence without readable support | Establish the source, select a passage, retain attribution |
| Continuous bouncing or pulsing | Competes with reading and can trivialize serious material | Use one purposeful emphasis event, then stop |
| A morph presented as historical truth | Suggests continuity or causality that may not exist | Add dates and categories; use a cut when appropriate |
| Tiny charts within busy collages | Prevents inspection while pretending to provide precision | Enlarge the chart or simplify the question |
| Sound effects on every change | Gives equal emphasis to unequal events | Reserve audible punctuation for meaningful events after a listening review |
| Copying the reference's branding | Transfers surface identity instead of design reasoning | Create original type, palette, assets, and subject-specific metaphors |

## 16. Creative quality checklist

Use this as a review conversation, not a mechanical score that can excuse a serious flaw.

### Meaning and evidence

- Can the scene's takeaway be stated in one accurate sentence?
- Does the visual show that claim, or merely contain related objects?
- Are facts, attributed claims, hypotheses, and illustrations distinguishable?
- Does a connector mean sequence, association, supply, movement, or causation—and is that meaning justified?
- Are dates, denominators, units, categories, and geographic statuses explicit where necessary?
- Has simplification removed anything that would materially change the conclusion?

### Composition and art direction

- Is there one primary reading task at each beat?
- Are the first, busiest, and final states individually readable?
- Do labels remain near their referents?
- Is empty space available for the next action?
- Does asset detail support recognition rather than compete with the explanation?
- Are source materials distinguishable from editorial overlays?
- Do palette, typography, texture, and depth behave consistently within their roles?

### Motion and transitions

- What remains invariant?
- What does each movement communicate?
- Are dependent events presented in an understandable order?
- Is simultaneous motion helping a relationship or dividing attention?
- Does the resolved state hold long enough for recognition, inference, and reading?
- Does the transition preserve a meaningful element or clearly announce a new context?
- Could a direct cut communicate the change more cleanly?
- Does animation create any false implication of quantity, physical behavior, chronology, or causality?

### Editorial and audio review

- Is the chosen visual mode appropriate to the current claim?
- Does the sequence vary cognitive work across the film?
- Are important comparisons available together rather than only in memory?
- When actual narration is available, does it reinforce rather than contradict the visible event?
- Have source-specific audio claims been verified by listening?
- Does the conclusion return to a model or question the viewer now understands more deeply?

### Approval standard

A sequence is ready when it is **clear enough to interpret, specific enough to feel intentional, accurate enough to trust, and restrained enough that its motion serves the idea**. Attractive styling cannot compensate for an ambiguous comparison, unsupported causal arrow, or unreadable source.

## 17. Compact training brief for another AI agent

You are directing an original editorial explainer. Learn the reasoning grammar of the supplied references, not their branding.

Begin with the viewer's question. Choose a visual representation that exposes the relevant relationship. Establish a recognizable object, place, source, or comparison. Preserve an invariant while changing what the viewer understands. Stage entities before relations and relations before conclusions. Use detail for recognition and evidence; use simplification for comparison and mechanism. Keep category colors, units, dates, and labels consistent. Give resolved states time to be understood. Connect scenes through the argument, using continuous transformations only when they help explain it. Prefer direct cuts when a change of evidence or historical context should be clear. Treat source material as evidence, not texture. Distinguish observation, interpretation, hypothesis, and illustration. Do not invent easing, narration synchronization, sound effects, or factual certainty absent from the evidence.

For each scene, deliver: proposition, evidence status, visual concept, initial composition, invariant, beat-by-beat choreography, motion personality, reading hold, narration relationship, transition logic, and failure checks. Reject a design whose only justification is that it looks dynamic. The viewer should finish each sequence able to explain a relationship they could not see at its start.
